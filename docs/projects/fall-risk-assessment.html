Fall Risk Assessment Using Gait Analysis and Wearable Sensors

How much clinical fall-risk discrimination survives a single accelerometer and a classifier light enough to run in real time? Research · submitted

The most useful column in the results table is execution time — and it is the one most papers omit.

Problem

Falls are a leading cause of injury and death among older adults. Identifying who is at risk before a fall happens is what enables prevention, but the clinical instruments that do it — Timed Up and Go, Berg Balance Scale, supervised gait assessment — are time-consuming, resource-heavy, and observe a person for minutes in a controlled room.

Wearable inertial sensors can observe gait continuously instead. Most of the published literature, though, buys accuracy with instrumentation: five or ten sensors across the body, or a deep network that cannot run on an embedded device. Both defeat the purpose — an obtrusive rig is not worn daily, and a model that needs a GPU is not a wearable.

This study constrains itself to a single lower-back accelerometer and measures the accuracy-versus-cost trade-off explicitly rather than reporting accuracy alone.

Dataset

The Long Term Movement Monitoring (LTMM) database from PhysioNet — public tri-axial accelerometer and gyroscope recordings from the lower back of 71 community-dwelling older adults (mean age 78.4 ± 4.7, range 65–87), worn on a belt. Participants are labelled fallers or non-fallers from self-reported falls over the preceding year, two or more falls defining a faller. Only the laboratory-recorded portion was used, for measurement standardisation and reproducibility.

Results

On the held-out test set, the two best models are separated by more than three orders of magnitude in execution time and by 0.3 accuracy points. That gap is the paper's actual finding: on this task, the deep model buys nothing it can keep once deployment is a constraint.

For fall risk specifically, recall on the faller class is the metric that matters — a missed faller is the costly error. SVM, KNN and the deep network all reach roughly 91 % F1 on the fall class. The Gonzalez segmentation algorithm gave the best balance of discrimination and simplicity.

My contribution

Stated separately from bibliographic authorship: I am the second author of W. Dib, A. Boudia, F. Boukhedimi, O. Kerdjidj.

  • Implementation and comparative evaluation of the four gait-cycle segmentation algorithms on the LTMM signals.
  • Signal pre-processing from a single inertial measurement unit — detrending, filtering, gravity removal.
  • Per-cycle feature extraction: statistical, frequency-domain and spatio-temporal gait parameters.
  • The classifier benchmark: dimensionality reduction, hyperparameter search, and the accuracy/latency evaluation.

What I took from it

  • The segmentation step sets the ceiling. Four algorithms on the same signal produce measurably different gait parameters; choosing one is a modeling decision, not preprocessing hygiene.
  • Report the cost next to the score.
  • Class-conditional metrics, not accuracy. In fall risk, a false negative and a false positive are not the same mistake.
